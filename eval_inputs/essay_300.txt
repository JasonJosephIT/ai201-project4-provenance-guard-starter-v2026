When I was twelve my family moved from a city apartment to a house at the edge of a small town, and the first thing I noticed was the quiet. There were no buses, no sirens, no neighbors arguing through the wall. At night I could hear the creek behind the house and, sometimes, a dog barking two farms away. For the first month I could not sleep. I kept waiting for a noise that never came.
School was harder than I expected. Everyone had known each other since kindergarten, and I was the kid who said "soda" when everyone else said "pop." I ate lunch alone for most of the fall. Then in November a boy named Daniel asked if I wanted to help him fix a bike he had found in a ditch. We spent three Saturdays in his garage with a borrowed wrench and a can of oil, and by the end the bike still did not work, but I had a friend.
Looking back, I think the move taught me two things. The first is that loneliness is usually temporary, even when it feels permanent. The second is that people often make friends by doing something together rather than by talking. Daniel and I barely spoke during those Saturdays. We just handed each other tools and complained about the rust.
I still live in a city now, and I like the noise again. But every so often, when I cannot sleep, I think about that creek and that broken bike, and the quiet does not seem so strange anymore. It seems like the place where I learned to wait for things to get better.
